Lunges: Forward vs Reverse, Muscles Worked and Your Knees

Updated November 25, 2026 · 14 minute read · Free PDF

Lunges are a strength exercise for one leg at a time: you step one foot forward or back, lower your back knee toward the floor, and stand up again. They work the front of your thighs hardest, then your glutes. Which way you step changes the work: in lab studies, a forward lunge loaded the knee more than a reverse lunge, and it worked the hip harder too. Here is which lunge to pick for your goal, how to do each one, what lunges do to your knees, the muscles worked, lunges vs squats, and a 6 week plan.

Which lunge should you do?

Pick by what you want. Each row comes from the studies in the sections below.

The side lunge has its own section: it works the knee and ankle more, and it is the hardest of these on the kneecap when you go deep. Whatever your goal, the reverse lunge is the one to learn first.

The reverse lunge: start here

Step back instead of forward. In a 2021 study, 20 women lunged forward and backward to the same depth: the reverse lunge put less force through the quads and the kneecap joint, and it loaded the knee more slowly (Goulette and colleagues, 2021). In a 2015 study of 9 men, the knee worked less in a reverse lunge than in a single-leg squat, and the authors put it first in a program for the knee: reverse lunge, then forward lunge, then single-leg squat (Comfort and colleagues, 2015).

  1. Stand tall. Feet hip width apart, hands on your hips or by your sides.
  2. Step back. Take a long step back with one foot and land on the ball of that foot. Your front foot stays flat.
  3. Lower. Bend both knees and drop straight down until your back knee is just above the floor.
  4. Keep your shape. Chest up, leaning a little forward. Your front knee points the same way as your toes, not inward.
  5. Stand up. Push through your front foot and bring the back foot in beside it. That is one lunge.
  6. Switch legs. Alternate, or do all your reps on one leg, then the other.

How long a step? In a 2025 study, the step that kept the front shin upright at the bottom was about 77 cm (2.5 feet) for women and 86 cm (2.8 feet) for men, from the back toe to the front heel. Half that step sent the front knee 8 to 10 cm (3 to 4 inches) past the toes (Escamilla and colleagues, 2025). How deep? In that study the back knee went down until it just touched the floor. If your knee complains, stop higher: the load on the kneecap rises the deeper you go (Escamilla and colleagues, 2008).

The forward lunge: more work for the knee and the hip

Step forward onto your heel, lower until your back knee is just above the floor, then push off the front foot back to standing. Your front leg has to catch and stop your body, and the numbers show it. In the 2021 study, the forward lunge put more force through the quads and the kneecap joint than the reverse lunge, and it loaded the knee faster (Goulette and colleagues, 2021). In the 2015 study it also made the hip work harder than the reverse lunge did (Comfort and colleagues, 2015). And in 32 young adults, a long forward lunge, a step of 60% of their height, made the biggest demands on the hip extensors, the glutes and hamstrings, of the lunges tested (Riemann and colleagues, 2013).

So the popular line that reverse lunges work the glutes more is not what was measured: the one study we found that measured the hip in both lunges found more hip work in the forward lunge. The reverse lunge's real advantage is your knee. Two things make the hip work harder:

  • A long step. In 20 people lunging with dumbbells, a long step made the quads, glutes, hamstrings, inner thigh and calves work harder than a short step (Escamilla and colleagues, 2025).
  • Your chest a little forward. In 10 adults, leaning forward raised the work at the hip and the activity of the gluteus maximus and the hamstrings. Leaning back changed nothing (Farrokhi and colleagues, 2008).

Walking and jumping lunges

A walking lunge strings forward lunges together: instead of pushing back, you bring the back foot through into the next lunge. Stepping made the glutes work much harder than keeping the feet planted. In 12 trained women holding 30% of their body weight, the gluteus maximus worked at 54% of its maximum in forward lunges and 58% in walking lunges, against 31% in a static lunge with both feet in place. Walking lunges also worked the lower back hardest: 40%, against 24 to 26% (Bezerra and colleagues, 2021).

In a 6 week study of 32 young soccer players, walking lunges twice a week made their hamstrings stronger, and jumping lunges made their 30 metre (98 foot) sprints faster (Jönhagen and colleagues, 2009). A jumping lunge switches legs in the air: from the bottom of a lunge, jump, swap your feet, and land softly in a lunge on the other leg.

To work the side of your hip, hold one dumbbell in the hand opposite the stepping leg. In 28 men lunging forward that way, the gluteus medius, the muscle on the side of the hip, worked at 90% of its maximum while lowering (Stastny and colleagues, 2015).

Lunges also help older walkers. In 11 people aged 54 to 88 who already walked at least 5,000 steps a day, adding lunges and walking downstairs or downhill to their walks for 8 weeks raised their knee strength by 19% and their chair stands in 30 seconds by 24%, and cut their sway with eyes closed by 45%. In the 4 weeks before, walking alone had changed none of these (Katsura and colleagues, 2024). In 20 middle-aged women, 8 weeks of lunges three times a week built leg muscle and steadier balance (Lee and Kim, 2022).

The side lunge

Step out to the side, bend that knee and sit your hips back while the other leg stays straight, then push back to standing. It works the leg in another direction: in the 2013 study, side lunges bent the ankle more than forward lunges and put more of the work on the knee and ankle muscles (Riemann and colleagues, 2013).

It is also the hardest of these on the kneecap when you go deep. In a 2022 study of 16 people, the side lunge put more force on the kneecap than the forward lunge from 40 degrees of knee bend down to 100, and an earlier study found the same at 80 to 90 degrees. Near the top, the forward lunge loaded it more (Escamilla and colleagues, 2008 and 2022). If your knees are touchy, keep side lunges shallow.

Do lunges hurt your knees?

Lunges load the knee, and you decide how much. These are the five dials the studies found:

DepthThe deeper the lunge, the more load on the kneecap (Escamilla and colleagues, 2008 and 2022)
DirectionForward loads the kneecap more than reverse (Goulette and colleagues, 2021). Side loads it most when deep
Step lengthNear the bottom, a short step loaded the kneecap more than a long one. Near the top, the long step did (Escamilla and colleagues, 2008 and 2022)
SteppingStepping out and back added load near the top, compared with a lunge with the feet in place (Escamilla and colleagues, 2008)
A low stepLunging up onto a 10 cm (4 inch) platform loaded the kneecap less than lunging on the floor (Escamilla and colleagues, 2022)

Can your knee go past your toes?

Yes, if it feels fine. It changes which joint does the work, not whether lunges are safe. In 25 women doing forward lunges with the same step, letting the knee travel past the toes raised the force in the quads and the stress on the patellar tendon, below the kneecap (Zellmer and colleagues, 2019). Holding the front shin upright moves the load elsewhere: in 18 people, an upright shin lowered the stress in the front knee but raised it in the back knee, and the back knee carried more total kneecap stress than the front one in every version (Hofmann and colleagues, 2017).

What about the ligaments?

The ACL, the knee ligament that sports injuries often tear, is hardly loaded by lunges. A model built from 18 people lunging with weights found no ACL force at all in forward and side lunges, and only a little, up to 50 newtons (the weight of about 5 kg), near the top of a short forward lunge with a step. The PCL at the back of the knee takes more, up to 765 newtons (about 78 kg), most in a long forward lunge (Escamilla and colleagues, 2010). That matters only while a PCL injury heals: ask your physiotherapist.

If your knee hurts

Turn the dials down: a reverse lunge, a long step, and only as deep as feels fine, or lunge up onto a low step. One research team suggests building up in this order: a shallow lunge, then a half lunge, then a full lunge with a long step up onto a 10 cm platform, then a full lunge on the floor (Escamilla and colleagues, 2022). Pain that stays, or a knee that swells, needs a physiotherapist, not more lunges.

Lunges muscles worked

Muscle sensors on the skin read each muscle's activity as a share of its maximum. In lunges the quads come first, then the glutes, and the hamstrings work least. The 2025 numbers below are with dumbbells heavy enough for 12 reps, so lunges with body weight alone read lower:

Quads, front of the thighThe hardest working muscles in both studies that measured them next to the glutes (Muyor and colleagues, 2020; Escamilla and colleagues, 2025). Pushing back up from a long forward lunge, the inner and outer quads reached 97 to 99% of their maximum
Gluteus maximus, your bottom48 to 64% on the way up (Escamilla and colleagues, 2025), and 54 to 58% in stepping and walking lunges (Bezerra and colleagues, 2021). It works harder in the front leg than the back one (Marchetti and colleagues, 2018)
Gluteus medius, side of the hip13 to 27% (Escamilla and colleagues, 2025), and 90% with a dumbbell in the opposite hand (Stastny and colleagues, 2015)
Inner thighThe adductor longus: 10 to 33% (Escamilla and colleagues, 2025)
Hamstrings, back of the thigh8 to 31%, more with a long step (Escamilla and colleagues, 2025). Last of six leg muscles in a 2020 study (Muyor and colleagues, 2020)
Calves15 to 29% (Escamilla and colleagues, 2025)
Lower back20% with the chest upright, 40% leaning forward (Bezerra and colleagues, 2021)

So lunges are a quads and glutes exercise. For the hamstrings, the step length matters most: the outer hamstring worked at 18% of its maximum on the way down from a long step, against 8% from a short one (Escamilla and colleagues, 2025).

Lunges vs squats

Both build your legs. The difference is one leg or two, and that decides what you get better at:

Muscle growthThe same. A 2025 review of 9 trials found no difference in muscle growth between one-leg and two-leg training (Kassiano and colleagues, 2025)
StrengthYou get stronger at what you train. Two-leg training raised two-leg strength more, one-leg training one-leg strength (Kassiano and colleagues, 2025; Xin and colleagues, 2026)
Jumping off one legOne-leg training wins, in a 2026 review of 24 trials (Xin and colleagues, 2026)
Sprints and changing directionNo clear difference (Xin and colleagues, 2026). Rugby players gained as much from split squats with the back foot raised as from back squats in 5 weeks (Speirs and colleagues, 2016)
Load on the kneeHigher in the lunge. In computer models built from about 50 young men, the front knee of a forward lunge peaked at 7.2 times body weight, each knee in a deep squat at 4.5 (De Roeck and colleagues, 2020)

So do both if you can: for squats, see 100 squats a day. A split squat, a lunge with both feet staying in place, sits in between: it is easier on the kneecap near the top, but the glutes work less on the way up than in a lunge that steps out and back, 48% of their maximum against 64% (Escamilla and colleagues, 2008 and 2025).

How many lunges? Sets, reps and a 6 week plan

This guide counts reps per leg: 3 sets of 12 means 12 on each leg in every set. RankTime counts every lunge, so 12 a leg, alternating, shows 24.

The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 review of 137 reviews found that strength grows with 2 or more sessions a week and 2 to 3 sets an exercise, and muscle with 10 or more sets a week for each muscle group. Light loads built muscle as well as heavy ones, though heavy loads built more strength, and sets did not need to go to failure (Currier and colleagues, 2026). So body weight is enough to start, as long as the last reps of each set are hard.

WeekLungeEach session
1Reverse3 sets of 8 a leg
2Reverse3 sets of 10 a leg
3Reverse3 sets of 12 a leg
4Forward3 sets of 10 a leg
5Forward3 sets of 12 a leg
6Forward4 sets of 12 a leg
  • Three days a week, with a day off in between. Week 6 makes 12 sets a week, past the 10 or more that the review linked with more muscle.
  • Rest 1:30 between sets, inside the range in our guide on how long to rest between sets. Alternate legs within each set.
  • Knees complain? Stay with reverse lunges and a long step, and go a little deeper each week, as in the order above.
  • Too easy? Hold dumbbells or wear a loaded backpack. The load on the knee rises with the weight you hold, and it was lower with the weight in front of your chest or in the hand opposite the front leg than with dumbbells at your sides (Hagen and colleagues, 2023). Add a little at a time.

Common questions

Are lunges better than squats?

Neither. One-leg and two-leg training built the same muscle in a 2025 review, and each made people stronger at its own movement. Do both if you can.

How many lunges should you do a day?

You do not need lunges every day. Two or three sessions a week of 2 to 3 sets build strength. When the total number of sets was the same, training a muscle once a week or more than 5 days a week built the same muscle (Currier and colleagues, 2026).

Are 10 lunges 10 on each leg?

Plans write it both ways. In this guide, yes: reps are per leg. If a plan gives one total, such as 20 alternating lunges, that is 10 a leg. RankTime counts every lunge, so 10 a leg shows 20.

Are reverse lunges better than forward lunges?

For your knees, yes: less load on the kneecap. For the hip, no: the forward lunge worked it harder. Start with reverse lunges and add forward ones, as in the plan.

Why do I wobble in a lunge?

Stand with your feet hip width apart, as if on two rails, not on one line. With the feet on one line, the front foot swayed from side to side about a quarter more in a 2018 study, 19 cm against 15 (Marchetti and colleagues, 2018). Holding your arms out in front makes balancing harder too (Wilson and colleagues, 2008).

What about curtsy lunges?

A curtsy lunge steps one foot back and across, behind the front leg. We found no study that measured it, so we cannot tell you what it does better than the lunges above.

Does RankTime count walking lunges?

Its camera needs your whole body in the frame, and walking lunges carry you out of it. Lunge on the spot, back to standing each time.
