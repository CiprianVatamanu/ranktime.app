How to Do a Handstand: Beginner Steps, Hold Times and Safety

Updated November 27, 2026 · 15 minute read · Free PDF

A handstand is balancing upside down on your hands with your body straight. You learn it in steps: arms straight overhead, weight on your hands, a hold against a wall, a safe way down, the kick-up, and then balance away from the wall. The balancing is done mostly by your fingers and wrists, and it takes practice: even circus students in the US held a handstand for about 10 seconds on average. Here are the six steps, the balance rules from lab studies of gymnasts, how long people really hold one, what handstands do and do not do for you, and who should be careful.

How do you do a handstand for beginners?

In six steps. Start at the first one you cannot pass yet, and move on when you can do what the last column says.

StepDrillMove on at
1. Arms upThumbs to the wall behind youElbows locked, back flat
2. HandsStraight-arm plank, then pike30 seconds each
3. WallWalk up the wall, chest to it60 seconds
4. Way downTurn out to the sideBoth sides, every time
5. Kick upKick up, heels to the wall5 in a row, legs together
6. FreeAway from the wall10 seconds, then 30
  1. Arms up. Stand with your head, back and bottom against a wall and raise both arms straight up until your thumbs touch the wall above your head. In a handstand your arms are in line with your body, about 180 degrees from your sides, with the elbows straight (Malíř and colleagues, 2024). If your low back has to leave the wall to get your thumbs there, the arch is doing what your shoulders cannot yet: keep practising this one, slowly, until it does not.
  2. Weight on your hands. Hold a plank on straight arms, the top of a push-up, for 30 seconds. Then the pike: from the plank, walk your feet in and lift your hips high until your shoulders are over your hands, and hold that for 30 seconds. Spread your fingers and press your whole hand into the floor.
  3. Chest to the wall. Start in the plank with your feet against a wall behind you. Walk your feet up the wall and your hands in toward it, a little at a time, until your body is straight, chest and toes facing the wall. Get your hands as close to the wall as you can. Come down the same way, walking your hands out. Build up to a minute.
  4. The way down. Before you kick up away from a wall, learn to get out: lift one hand, turn your body toward that side and step down, the way a cartwheel lands. Practise it from small kick-ups on a soft floor, to both sides, until you do it without thinking. Rolling forward out of a handstand is another way down: learn the roll on its own first.
  5. Kick up. Face the wall, hands on the floor a short way from it, one foot forward. Swing your back leg up, push off the front one and bring it to the first, until your heels touch the wall. Arms stay straight. Kick just hard enough to arrive, not to bang into the wall.
  6. Free. Kick up away from the wall, or kick up to it and ease your heels off, and balance with your fingers, as below. 10 seconds free is longer than the average circus student held in a US study. 30 and 60 seconds are what USA Gymnastics asks of young girls in its talent test.

How do you hold a handstand?

With your fingers, mostly. In a lab study of 12 experienced gymnasts, the wrists did the balancing more than 75% of the time in a handstand, the way the ankles do more than 90% of the time when you stand (Blenkinsop and colleagues, 2017). A 2025 review of 21 lab studies of the handstand reached the same view: gymnasts balance at the wrists and keep the rest of the body in one fixed shape, and the stronger ones held their handstands longer (MacDonald and colleagues, 2025). Six rules follow from the studies.

FingersPress your fingertips into the floor when your legs start to tip over, and the heels of your hands when you start to come back down. In gymnasts, the forearm muscles that do this worked hardest of all: 60% of their maximum on average, more than any other muscle measured (Kochanowicz and colleagues, 2018)
ElbowsKeep them locked. When gymnasts could not keep their balance at the wrists, their elbows bent, and a bent elbow threw them badly off balance (Gautier and colleagues, 2007)
HipsKeep them still. The best handstands leaned on the wrists and shoulders, with little from the hips, while the hips took over in the shaky ones (Kerwin and Trewartha, 2001). Less expert gymnasts moved more at the hips too (Gautier and colleagues, 2009)
HeadLook at the floor between your hands. Five expert gymnasts balanced much better with their normal head position, or the head tipped further back, than with the head in line with the body or the chin tucked toward the chest (Asseman and Gahéry, 2005)
EyesKeep them open. Balance got worse with the eyes closed in the same study and in others (Gautier and colleagues, 2007)
Fresh armsStop each hold while it is still steady. Tired arms made 14 acrobatic gymnasts sway more, mostly from side to side (Sabido and colleagues, 2024)

How do you do a handstand against a wall?

Two ways, and they train different things. A wall handstand hold is the safest way to spend time upside down: the wall does the balancing while you build the straight line and the strength to hold it.

Chest to wallBack to wall
Getting upWalk your feet up the wallKick up, facing the wall
Facing the wallYour chest and toesYour back and heels
Coming downWalk your hands outStep down, or turn out
It trainsStart here: the straight lineThe kick-up

Start with your chest to the wall: you only go as far up as your strength takes you, and you come down the same way you went up. Walking up and back down like this is the wall walk. Use your back to the wall to practise kick-ups, and keep your ribs in: with your heels on the wall and your hands away from it, it is easy to hang there in an arch, which is a fault in a gymnastics handstand (USA Gymnastics, 2026).

How long does it take to learn a handstand?

Longer than most articles promise, and no study has timed adults learning one from zero. Here is what the research does show:

A semester was not enough111 physical education students in Prague, aged 19 to 23 and all active athletes, had a semester's gymnastics course that teaches the handstand, plus two 45 minute lessons on it. Asked to hold a handstand for 2 seconds, most could not do it cleanly (Malíř and colleagues, 2024)
Young gymnasts: about 2 yearsHolding a handstand still, with the body in one fixed shape, can normally be reached within 2 years of frequent training, a 2025 review notes (MacDonald and colleagues, 2025)
Circus artists still wobble201 circus artists across the US, students and professionals who trained about 7 to 9 times a week, held a handstand for 10 to 14 seconds on average (Greenspan and Stuckey, 2024)

So plan in months for a free handstand, and use the wall steps to make progress you can measure along the way: the plan below builds to a 60 second wall hold in 6 weeks.

Not long. These are the numbers from real tests that we could find:

The circus test stopped at 60 seconds, and its averages hide a wide spread, from 0 to the full 60 (Greenspan and Stuckey, 2024). The 2 seconds are the men's gymnastics rule for held positions (Malíř and colleagues, 2024). USA Gymnastics' TOPs test asks for a kick-up with the fingers facing forward and no walking on the hands, and takes points off for an arch, a pike, the head out, bent arms or legs, and feet apart (USA Gymnastics, 2026). So 10 seconds free puts you past the average circus student, and a minute is what talented 9 and 10 year old gymnasts are tested on.

What is the handstand world record?

1 hour, 15 minutes and 13 seconds, the longest time balancing on the hands, by Néstor Santiago Gonzalez Valero in Bogotá, Colombia, on 2 August 2026, much of it on one arm. Guinness World Records counts any position in which only the hands touch the ground.

What muscles do handstands work?

Mostly your forearms and shoulders. A handstand hold is one of the isometric exercises: your muscles work hard while your joints stay still. The muscle studies measured gymnasts in handstands with sensors on the skin (Kochanowicz and colleagues, 2018 and 2019).

ForearmsThe wrist flexors, on the palm side of the forearm, do the balancing: 60% of their maximum on average in a handstand on the floor, the most of any muscle measured. Each hand carries about half your weight, 0.47 times it in 15 breakdancers measured with pressure sensors (Tsuda and colleagues, 2026), mostly through the heel of the hand: in 15 former college gymnasts, 47% of the force went through its little finger side and 37% through its thumb side (Scigliano and colleagues, 2024)
Upper trapeziusThe muscle between your neck and shoulder, which lifts the shoulders toward the ears with the arms overhead: 20 to 26% of all the muscle work recorded
Front shouldersThe anterior deltoids: 15 to 18%
TricepsThey keep the elbows straight: 13 to 16%
The restThe other eight muscles measured, from the lats and the chest to the abs, glutes and thighs, shared what was left, about a quarter or less

Practice makes it cheaper: adult gymnasts used about a fifth less effort in their triceps and front shoulders than teenage gymnasts did (Kochanowicz and colleagues, 2019).

Do handstands build muscle?

No study has measured it. The studies above show which muscles work while you balance, and how hard, not what they gain over months of practice.

Are handstands good for you?

For your handstand balance, yes, and likely for your shoulders and forearms. For the rest of what you may read, the research is thin or missing:

Stronger shoulders and forearmsLikely, but not tested. They work hard in a handstand, as above. No study has trained people with handstands and measured the gain
Better balanceOn your hands, yes. Expert gymnasts balance better than less expert ones in the studies of the 2025 review. Standing balance is another matter: in 15 elite gymnasts, the best balancers on their hands were not the best at standing still (Asseman and colleagues, 2004)
Blood to the brain, slower ageingNot shown. The only report is of one man in China, 66, who had used a handstand machine for 40 years and had healthy blood vessels in his brain (Liu and colleagues, 2022). One person and no comparison cannot show that the handstands did it
Hair growthNo evidence. We searched the research on 27 November 2026 and found no study of handstands, headstands or the "inversion method" and hair
Taller, or a decompressed spineNo evidence. No study has measured height after handstands. In X-rays of 19 gymnasts, the low back flattened and the pelvis tucked under in a handstand (Eyssartier and colleagues, 2024). Hanging upside down on an inversion device does stretch the low back (Ballantyne and colleagues, 1986), but in a handstand your spine holds up your hips and legs

Are handstands bad for you?

They can be, in three places: your wrists, your eyes and, while you are upside down, your blood pressure. Falls are the other risk: learn step 4, the way down, before you kick up away from a wall, and practise on a soft floor with space around you.

Are handstands bad for your wrists?

Your wrists take the load, and pain is common. In a survey of 321 adults who practise handstands at least once a week, mostly in yoga, capoeira and movement training, 57% had wrist pain that kept coming back or never went, more often the younger ones. Hours of practice, warm-ups, wrist braces and grip aids made no clear difference (Martonovich and colleagues, 2025). The authors suspect that how hard you train and how the wrist is loaded matter more than the hours. So build your hold times up slowly, as the steps do, spread your fingers and press through the whole hand, and stop for the day when a wrist hurts. Pain that stays is one for a doctor or a physiotherapist.

Are handstands bad for your eyes?

Going upside down raises the pressure inside your eyes for as long as you stay there. In 75 experienced yoga practitioners, eye pressure doubled as soon as they went into a headstand, up by about 15 mmHg, and was still up 5 minutes later (Baskaran and colleagues, 2006). Hanging upside down, it reached 35 to 40 mmHg, and 11 of 19 eyes showed gaps in their field of vision that went away afterwards (Sanborn and colleagues, 1987). After head-down yoga poses, it was back to normal within 2 minutes (Jasien and colleagues, 2015). With glaucoma it matters more: in one patient, daily headstands were linked with the glaucoma getting worse (Gallardo and colleagues, 2006). Straining is the other risk. Eye doctors have reported bleeding inside the eye after a handstand: in a 15 year old after a 10 second handstand in a school PE class, whose eye healed in 4 months (Miyaki and colleagues, 2020), and in an earlier report of sight loss (Oliphant and colleagues, 2014). Keep breathing while you are up. If you have glaucoma, high eye pressure or a retina problem, or have had eye surgery, ask your eye doctor before going upside down.

Are handstands bad for your brain?

We found no study that links handstands with strokes or brain damage. What has been measured is blood pressure upside down. Hanging on inversion devices, it rose by about 20 mmHg on average in 18 young adults (Ballantyne and colleagues, 1986), and in a trial with 60 young women, 2 minutes upside down raised both numbers, and the researchers advised that people with high blood pressure may need to avoid inversion (Haskvitz and Hanten, 1986). A handstand lasts seconds, not minutes, but if you have high blood pressure or heart disease, or have had a stroke, ask your doctor first.

Can you do handstands every day?

Many people do, and no study sets a limit. In the survey of 321 adults, more hours a week did not mean more wrist pain, but pain was common at every level (Martonovich and colleagues, 2025). So practise as often as your wrists stay happy. The plan below uses four short sessions a week, never more than two days in a row, and keeps every hold short of shaking.

Train Monday, Tuesday, Thursday and Friday: start with the step 1 check and 30 seconds of the pike, then 5 to 6 minutes of wall holds, then the kick work. The turn-outs are step 4, from small kick-ups. Test on day 1 and at the end of week 6: your longest chest-to-wall hold, and your longest free one. If a hold breaks before its time, end it there and repeat the week. Not at step 3 yet? Spend your first weeks on steps 1 and 2: 3 rounds of 30 seconds of the plank and the pike.

Common questions

Why can't I do a handstand?

Find the first of the six steps above that you cannot pass yet: that is the one to work on. And give it time: in the study of 111 sports students, a semester of lessons was not enough for most of them.

Is a handstand calisthenics?

Yes. Calisthenics is training with your own body weight, and the handstand is one of its skills. To build a calisthenics workout around it, add pulling and pushing: see our guides on how to do a pull-up and on dips.

Is a headstand easier than a handstand?

A headstand stands on a wider base, the head and both hands, closer to the floor, and a small base with a high centre of mass is what makes a handstand hard, the 2025 review explains. The cost of a headstand is your neck: in 45 yoga practitioners, the head carried 40 to 48% of their body weight at its peak (Hector and Jensen, 2015).

Can you do handstands while pregnant?

Ask your ob-gyn or midwife. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists advises avoiding activities that may result in a fall during pregnancy, gymnastics among them, because the growing belly shifts your balance (ACOG, 2024).
